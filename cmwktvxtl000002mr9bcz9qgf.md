To be honest, I hesitated before writing about security. Still, I think what is happening in Japan right now should be written down. By [Trend Micro's count](https://newsroom.trendmicro.com/2026-10-19-Trend-Micro-Announces-Up-to-JPY10-Billion-toward-Cyber-Resilience-Initiative-to-Strengthen-Security-for-Japanese-Organizations-in-the-AI-Era) ([Japanese original](https://www.trendmicro.com/ja_jp/jp-security/26/j/securitytrend-20261016-01.html)), organizations in Japan publicly disclosed 607 cybersecurity incidents in the first nine months of 2026, already more than the 594 for all of 2025. Many of the customers we support have already started reviewing their own defenses, and helping them is my team's top priority. What I feel most strongly in that work is that security is no longer a job for security specialists alone. That is also why I think there is value in someone who is not a security specialist writing this, and in writing about Japan in English. The aim of this post is to sort out, from the public record, what engineers and organizations can check in their own environments and how to prioritize the response. It does not judge the organizations that were affected, and it does not recommend a particular product.

To see what was happening, I went through the unauthorized-access and ransomware cases that organizations in Japan disclosed in the three months from August to October 2026, and sorted them by how the unauthorized parties got in. I expected the ways in to cluster in two places. One was flaws in public-facing applications. The other was getting past a perimeter device, such as a VPN appliance, into the internal network. The first guess was right, and the second was mostly wrong. Of the 240 cases whose notices stated the way in, only 3 went through a perimeter device. Among the cases that reached internal systems, the largest group used stolen credentials.

JPCERT/CC (Japan's coordination center for incident response) has already described the techniques in [an English blog post](https://blogs.jpcert.or.jp/en/2026/10/web_api_attacks.html) ([Japanese original](https://blogs.jpcert.or.jp/ja/2026/10/web_api_attacks.html)), which also reports that services for users outside Japan were targeted. What this post is after is not the techniques but the differences between organizations that the public record shows. Organizations differed widely in the time it took to notice an intrusion or data being taken. The same holds for the code you ship. Neither the time to notice an intrusion nor the time to notice a flaw you shipped can be shortened by the security team alone.

*Where I'm coming from.* At AWS, I am one of the leads of an initiative to support the security of our customers in Japan. AWS service names appear as concrete examples for readers who use AWS. The ideas themselves do not depend on a particular cloud or product. The views are my own, not my employer's.

> **TL;DR**
>
> 1. **The way in was not the perimeter device.** More than 70% of the 240 cases with a stated way in came through a flaw in a public-facing application or server, or through stolen credentials. Perimeter devices accounted for 3. Instead, some cases involved internal analytics tools and admin consoles through which hundreds of thousands of records may have been retrieved.
> 2. **The difference between organizations was the time to notice.** By their own notices, half noticed unauthorized access the same day or the next, but more than 20% took over 10 days. Nearly all of the late ones were quiet methods that took data without stopping the business, yet access through the same routes was noticed within a day elsewhere. The code you ship has the same lag. A change shipped between scheduled assessments goes unchecked unless someone owns that job.
> 3. **Start early, in the order the data shows.** In the next month, check your main public-facing applications, review authentication and permissions for internal tools, and put multi-factor authentication on privileged accounts. Also turn on logging and threat detection, pre-approve containment, and make backups immutable. In the next quarter, I suggest measuring an actual restore from those backups and starting a project to build checks into the development process. For the checks, which product you pick matters less than starting.

## The way in was not the perimeter device

First, the method. The scope is the unauthorized-access and ransomware cases that companies, universities, and public bodies in Japan disclosed themselves from August 1 to October 31, 2026. I took the cases from the daily lists of innovaTopia, a security news site that links each disclosure in Japan to the organization's notice ([August](https://innovatopia.jp/cyber-security/cyber-security-news/119066/), [September](https://innovatopia.jp/cyber-security/cyber-security-news/119067/), [October](https://innovatopia.jp/cyber-security/cyber-security-news/119068/), all in Japanese). I wrote the coding rules myself. AI agents applied them in parallel to every entry and, for 332 of the 365 cases counted below, to the organization's own notice. Scripts checked the output for missing fields and invalid values, and I corrected 15 entries by hand. Of the 574 entries in the lists, 452 remained after removing advisories, cases overseas, misdirected email and lost devices or documents, and cases reported only in the press.

Three counting rules apply. Unauthorized access to a shared platform that several organizations use counts as one case, even when each user organization disclosed it separately. I classified the way in only as the notice states it, without guessing. Days and record counts come only from the notices, and the record count is the largest figure each organization disclosed, including possible exposure, not a count of people. I do not publish a per-case list, because it could identify the organizations.

These rules leave 365 cases, broken down by the stated way in below.

| Way in | Cases (of 365) | Share of affected records |
| --- | --: | --: |
| A. Public-facing applications, APIs, and servers | 103 (28%) | 51% |
| B. Credentials and internal infrastructure | 96 (26%) | 3% |
| C. Vendors | 41 (11%) | 3% |
| D. Not stated in the notice | 125 (34%) | 42% |

Of the 240 cases with a stated way in, 199, or more than 80%, were A or B. A holds 51% of the records, and adding the D cases that hit customer-facing systems brings that to 85%. Without the single largest case, it is still 82%.

Of the 103 A cases, 58 notices described the method. The largest group, 22, used a vulnerability in third-party software the organization ran. Next came two groups with 11 each. One was flaws in how the organization's own code handled input (SQL injection, file uploads, and the like). The other was legitimate functions called at scale or in unintended ways (automated repeated access, abuse of password reset or account sign-up). Misconfigurations followed with 10. Only 4 stated an authorization flaw where specifying another user's ID returned that user's data (BOLA, first on the [OWASP API Security Top 10](https://api-security.owasp.org/editions/2023/en/0xa1-broken-object-level-authorization)). Function abuse and authorization flaws are both hard for web application firewall (WAF) rules to tell apart from normal requests, because only the application knows whether a request was allowed for that user.

Public-facing applications were not the only things reachable from outside. In 11 of the A cases, the way in was a system meant only for internal users, such as a data-analysis tool, an admin console, or a test environment. Seven of those were vulnerabilities in third-party software, and in 8, tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of records may have been retrieved. Under zero trust, internal systems are often meant to be usable from outside, so what protects them is what stands in front of them and what they can read. There are three things you can do. Require sign-in and multi-factor authentication in front of each tool, as with zero trust network access (ZTNA), so that requests that fail authentication do not reach it. Following the principle of least privilege, give it only the data it needs rather than read access to all production data. And patch it as fast as public-facing applications.

B also holds 13 ransomware cases whose notices did not say how the unauthorized parties got in. I placed them in B by what they hit. Of the other 83, 76 used stolen credentials, including taken-over email accounts. This is where my guess was wrong. From reading reports in earlier years, I had expected most ways into internal networks to be vulnerabilities in perimeter devices. Of the 3 perimeter-device cases, one used a vulnerability in the device and is counted as A, because the device is reachable from the internet. The other 2 were ransomware in B, one through a misused VPN account. Only 4 ransomware notices stated the way in, so half of those went through a perimeter device. Even if all 13 of the others had also gone through one, perimeter devices would account for 16 of the 240, under 7%. In all, B includes 17 ransomware cases, and some took several days to over 10 days to restore operations or services.

Of the 19 B cases that stated how the credentials were stolen, 13 were phishing and 6 were passwords exposed elsewhere and reused (credential stuffing). Another 5 were support scams that ended with a device under remote control, and 3 cases had multi-factor authentication in place and were still affected. On phishing, [Trend Micro](https://newsroom.trendmicro.com/2026-10-12-TrendAI-TM-Research-Reinforces-Japans-Urgent-Cybersecurity-Warning-as-Incidents-Surge-in-2026) ([Japanese original](https://www.trendmicro.com/ja_jp/about/newsroom/press-releases/2026/pr-20261013-01.html)) writes that AI lets unauthorized parties write natural text in languages they do not speak, weakening the protection the Japanese language used to provide. It also says it has not linked the recent cases in Japan to AI.

This count has limits. It covers only disclosed cases over three months, and it may lean toward consumer-facing industries. Cases by month were 69 in August, 77 in September, and 219 in October, and October's rise may partly reflect how the lists were compiled. Still, the Personal Information Protection Commission (Japan's data protection authority) also [received more reports of data exposure through unauthorized access](https://www.ppc.go.jp/files/pdf/261028_sankoushiryou-2.pdf) (in Japanese), from 303 in September to 830 in October through the 16th. Among the cases with a stated way in, A or B made up 93% in August, 91% in September, and 76% in October. I think that is enough to decide where to start.

## The difference between organizations was the time to notice

Many notices give the day the unauthorized access began, the day the organization noticed, and the day it disclosed. From noticing to disclosure, the median across the 208 cases with both dates was 4 days, and about 80% disclosed within 10 days. From the start of unauthorized access to noticing it (the interval usually called time to detect, or MTTD), the median across the 96 cases with both dates was 1 day. Comparing the same 96 cases, disclosure fell within 2 to 10 days for 60% of them. Time to notice instead split in two. Half noticed the same day or the next, and more than 20% took over 10 days. These times to notice probably understate the real ones. They count only what organizations noticed and disclosed themselves, from the first access each one found, leaving out earlier probing and access older than its logs.

Of the 21 cases that took more than 10 days to notice, 20 used quiet methods that viewed or took data without stopping the business. They included programs planted in payment pages, taken-over email accounts, and support scams. The other was ransomware at a vendor, whose effect took time to reach its client. The route alone did not make a case late, though. Of the 50 cases noticed the same day or the next, only 2 were ransomware. Most of the rest came through web applications or accounts, the same two routes as most of the late ones.

Quiet data removal is hard to notice unless someone watches the logs. And logs cannot be searched back unless they were turned on before they were needed. It sounds basic. My team reviews customer environments with tools such as the [Security Maturity Model](https://maturitymodel.security.aws.dev/en/) and [SATv2](https://github.com/awslabs/aws-security-assessment-solution) (an open-source tool that checks AWS account settings in bulk). Yet in those reviews, it is not uncommon to find access log retention periods or log levels that differ from what the customer expected.

The same lag exists in the code you ship. The fix for every A way in sat with the people who build and run the system, and how long it takes to notice a flaw after shipping it also varies by organization. Some customers my team works with outsource vulnerability assessments of their public-facing applications to outside firms. The assessments follow a yearly plan, and the contract sets the scope, so changes outside it, such as a refactoring that does not change any feature, have sometimes been left out. What this arrangement covers is the contracted scope, checked on the day of the assessment. Both the assessment firm and the customer are doing what the contract says. Even so, the job of noticing changes shipped between assessments belongs to nobody unless someone is assigned to it.

AI has changed this from two directions. One is the volume of code. At Spotify, monthly merged changes roughly doubled in a year ([a previous post](https://blog.simukappu.com/ai-driven-development-is-a-diagnostic-for-your-organization)). The other is the cost of checking. Reporting on Project Glasswing, an effort to find vulnerabilities in critical software with AI, [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/glasswing-initial-update) writes that progress on software security used to be limited by how fast new vulnerabilities could be found. Finding has become faster, and the constraint has moved to the side that receives the findings and fixes them. Some of the customers we support have also started checking all of their existing code at once with AI. A one-time full check, though, leaves the time until the next check as time to notice. The deciding question is whether that one-time check can be designed as the first round of checks that continue with every change.

## Start early, in the order the data shows

What follows is my proposal. I set the priorities mentioned at the start from the data so far. First come the most common ways in: public-facing applications, internal tools, and stolen credentials. Next come logs and detection, which shorten the time to notice, and then recovery from ransomware, which stops the business. Building checks into development runs alongside them from next quarter.

| When | Area | What to do | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Next month | Prevention | Start source code scanning (SAST) and testing of the running application (DAST or penetration testing) on your main public-facing applications. Require sign-in in front of internal tools, narrow the data permissions they hold to what they need, and patch them as fast as public-facing applications. Until patches and fixes land, use a WAF to cover known vulnerabilities and to rate-limit requests. Put phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication (such as passkeys) on admin consoles and privileged accounts | Can someone use another user's ID to access that user's data? Do requests without sign-in reach internal tools? Does every critical finding have an owner and a due date? |
| Next month | Detection and response | Turn on logging and threat detection for public endpoints, authentication, and cloud management operations in every account. Learn the usual request rate and mix of paths on your main public endpoints, and set up log checks for departures from them. Decide who may stop what at night or on a holiday without calling a meeting | Can you trace, today, a request and a management operation from 30 days ago? Would you notice such a departure today? Can you stop something without waiting for approval? |
| Next month | Recovery | Keep backups in immutable (write once, read many, or WORM) storage that even production administrator rights cannot delete | Can anyone delete a backup or shorten its retention? |
| Next quarter | Recovery | Actually restore one workload from those backups | Is there an established restore procedure that works under real conditions, and have the people who would run it practiced it? |
| From next quarter | Development | Start a project to build checks into the development process (shift left) | Does each finding from each change have an owner and a due date? |

A WAF mitigates many common kinds of unauthorized access but cannot replace fixing the application's own vulnerabilities. Noticing what it cannot stop takes logs. Start by confirming that logs are kept for public endpoints, cloud management operations, and the application itself. On AWS, the first two are the access logs of load balancers, Amazon CloudFront, and AWS WAF, plus AWS CloudTrail. With an AWS Organizations organization trail and Amazon S3 Object Lock (in compliance mode), you can keep them where even production administrator rights cannot delete them. Amazon GuardDuty analyzes management events, network flow records, and DNS query records even if you have not turned those logs on yourself, but it does not see inside the application. Only the application's own logs can record what happens there, such as an authorization decision.

For backups, AWS Backup Vault Lock in compliance mode keeps even the root user from deleting them before their retention ends. Probably more organizations than you would expect have not once tested a realistic restore from backup. Even organizations that plan for restores often build the procedure on continuous replication or on a dedicated line between on-premises and the cloud, assuming that the backups themselves are intact and that the line can stay connected. They have turned the first thing to suspect after an intrusion into an assumption.

Building checks into the development process is a project in its own right. One customer my team works with plans to go further. It is starting by replacing its scheduled outside assessments with AI-based checks, and aims for development teams to run the checks themselves across every product. Even at the planning stage, it has become clear that what holds the work back is more organizational than technical. The open questions are who triages and fixes the findings, who checks the quality of the fixes, how to keep costs down, and how to get the cooperation of the organizations that build the products. For cost, one approach is to scan only the difference from the last run instead of everything each time. Defenders have the source code and the design documents, which unauthorized parties do not. Checks that read them, such as SAST, code review, and penetration tests that take them as context, can run on every change, so they keep pace with today's faster shipping more easily than black-box checks from outside.

Starting early matters more than which product you pick for these checks.

More products now use AI for code scanning and penetration testing, including OpenAI's [Codex Security](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/security), Anthropic's [Claude Security](https://claude.com/product/claude-security), partner products, and cybersecurity models offered to vetted defenders. On AWS, AWS Security Agent (now part of AWS Continuum) can run penetration tests [from your CI/CD pipeline](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/securityagent/latest/userguide/cicd-pentest.html) after each deployment. What takes time to build is the handling of findings, and it starts only when the checks do.

When coding agents arrived, many organizations I work with started experimenting at once instead of waiting for the tools to settle. One of them rolled out three in about a year ([an earlier post](https://blog.simukappu.com/three-pivots-in-ai-driven-development)). The case for starting is stronger this time, because unauthorized parties are moving as well. Since late August 2026, [Google Threat Intelligence Group](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/artex-and-friends-enter-agentic-offensive-frameworks) has seen them use open-source AI agent frameworks built for penetration testing, such as ARTEX, in intrusions, and [Amazon Threat Intelligence](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/amazon-threat-intelligence-observes-threat-actors-running-cybercrime-campaigns-with-artex-ai-agents/) has described campaigns run on ARTEX. Both say the methods are not new and the agents make them faster. Neither ties the cases counted here to these tools. Changes keep shipping until the checks begin, and that interval gives unauthorized parties time to look for flaws from outside.

Each of these steps costs people's time and money, and pre-approved containment may sometimes stop a service that did not need to stop. I do not see these costs as a reason to weigh security against other work. Security comes first, and the costs only shape how it is done.

## Closing

Here is what this post cannot answer. The count covers only disclosed cases, and rightly so. It rests only on what organizations themselves made public. Only 96 of the 365 cases give the number of days it took to notice. And building checks fully into development is still in progress, even at the customers my team works with.

Even so, the public record already shows where to start. Any organization can try to measure both kinds of time to notice from its own records. If it cannot, it learns which records are missing. Your results may differ from my numbers, and that is fine. Finding out where the difference comes from is itself the next check.

To repeat, now that unauthorized parties can use AI too, security is no longer a job for security specialists alone. It is something every architect designs for and every software engineer owns in everyday work.

How many days would it take your organization to notice unauthorized access, and to notice a flaw it shipped? Is your work designed so that you can keep measuring both? I would like to help shorten those two times, even a little.
